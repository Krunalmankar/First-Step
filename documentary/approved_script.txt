Could the United States Collapse
Because of Its Debt?
Figures verified as of 11 October 2026. Fiscal year 2026 ran
from 1 October 2025 to 30 September 2026.

Cold open

Forty trillion dollars.
[pause]
That's how much the United States government owes right
now. More than $40 trillion, and still climbing.
In August 2026, America's debt crossed $40 trillion for the
first time in history. Just five months earlier, it was $39

trillion.
And here's the part most people don't realise. In the fiscal
year that just ended, America spent $1.14 trillion on interest
alone. That's more than its entire military. More than
Medicare. Only Social Security costs more.
Every single second, around $36,000 of American tax
money disappears into interest payments. Not into roads.
Not into schools. Just interest.
[pause]
So is this the beginning of the end? Or is it one of the most
overhyped fears in finance?
Stay with me, because the answer is more surprising than
you think.

Part 1: The machine that never stops
borrowing

Let's start simple.
Every year, the US government spends money on Social
Security, Medicare, defence, roads, schools, and interest on
the debt it already owes.
In fiscal year 2026, it spent $7.4 trillion. But it collected only
$5.4 trillion.
Think about that. For every $100 the government spent, it
brought in only about $73.

So it borrows the rest. Nearly $2 trillion in a single year.
That's about $5.5 billion, every single day.
It borrows by selling Treasury bonds to investors around the
world, and those investors lend America the money.
Sounds fine, right? Countries borrow all the time.
But here's where it turns dangerous.
That new borrowing adds to the debt. More debt means
more interest. In just one year, America's interest bill jumped
by $115 billion. More interest means even more borrowing
next year. And when old bonds come due, America doesn't
pay them off. It borrows again to repay them.
Today, more than one out of every five dollars the
government collects goes straight to interest.
Split evenly, the debt now works out to more than $115,000
for every man, woman and child in America.
It's a machine that feeds itself. And the faster it spins, the
harder it is to stop.

Part 2: The warning signs are already
here

Now, you might think, "Come on. It's America. Nobody
seriously doubts America."
Except... they already have.
In August 2011, a political fight over the debt ceiling pushed
the US so close to the edge that S&P stripped America of its

perfect AAA credit rating for the first time ever.
In August 2023, Fitch downgraded it too.
And in May 2025, Moody's, which had given America its top
rating for more than a century, finally cut it as well.
[pause]
For the first time in modern history, not one of the three
major rating agencies gives the United States a perfect
score.
And the bond market noticed.
In August 2026, the interest rate on America's 30-year bond
climbed to 5.31%. That's the highest since 2007, on the eve
of the global financial crisis. Just days earlier, the Treasury
had to offer 5.22% to sell new 30-year bonds, the highest at
one of those auctions since 2001.
In other words, the world is still lending to America. But it's
charging more.
That doesn't mean collapse is coming tomorrow. But it does
mean the people whose job is to judge risk are getting
nervous.

Part 3: Three ways this could go
terribly wrong

So how would a real crisis actually start? There are three
doors. And none of them are pleasant.
Door number one: the investors walk away.

Imagine one morning, global investors simply say: "We'll still
lend you money... but only at much higher interest rates."
Bond prices crash. Yields shoot up. Suddenly every dollar
America borrows costs more, every dollar it refinances costs
more, and mortgages, car loans and business loans all get
more expensive with it.
And remember, interest is already America's second-biggest
bill. The Congressional Budget Office projects it will reach
$2.1 trillion a year by 2036, even without a panic.
Think a sudden panic is far-fetched? In 2022, it happened to
the United Kingdom.
On 23 September 2022, the British government announced
£45 billion of unfunded tax cuts. Investors panicked. The
pound crashed to its lowest level against the dollar in history.
British bond markets went into freefall. Pension funds were
pushed to the edge of collapse, and the Bank of England had
to pledge up to £65 billion in an emergency rescue.
Less than a month later, the Prime Minister resigned.
[pause]
That was Britain. Now imagine it happening to the country
whose bonds the entire world depends on.
Door number two: the dollar slowly melts.
If the government leans on printing money to handle its debt,
the dollar quietly loses value. Prices rise. Savings shrink. And
foreign investors start asking a dangerous question: "Why
are we holding so many dollars?"

This one doesn't arrive with a crash. It creeps in. And by the
time people notice, it's already deep.
Is it already happening? Slowly. Around the year 2000, more
than 70% of the world's foreign-exchange reserves were
held in dollars. By mid-2026, that share had slipped to
56.7%.
Door number three: America defaults... on purpose.
This is the strangest one. America could have the money
and still fail to pay.
How? Politics.
By law, America can only borrow up to a set limit. Right now,
that limit is $41.1 trillion. And America is already within about
a trillion dollars of it. Independent estimates suggest the
ceiling could be hit sometime in 2027.
If Congress and the White House can't agree to raise it in
time, payments could be delayed. And the moment the
world's "safest" asset misses a payment, global markets
could panic.
Not because America is broke. But because America chose
to stumble.
[pause]
Now, here's something important. A crisis is not the same as
collapse. A country can go through a brutal financial shock
and still survive. But the Congressional Budget Office itself
has warned that rising debt raises the risk of exactly this kind
of crisis, and leaves less room to fight the next emergency.

Part 4: So why hasn't it happened yet?

Okay. If all this is true... why is America still standing?
Because it holds some of the strongest cards in the world.
First, the dollar is still king. Even after that slow decline, the
dollar makes up 56.7% of global foreign-exchange reserves,
according to the IMF. The euro, its closest rival, sits at about
20.6%. Nothing else comes close.
Second, America has a giant, productive economy, worth
more than $30 trillion a year, with huge companies, huge
innovation, and a massive tax base. Even last year, tax
revenue rose by $169 billion.
Third, America borrows in its own currency. It never has to
hunt for some foreign currency to pay its bills. That alone
protects it from the trap that has broken many other
countries.
And fourth, US Treasuries are the backbone of global
finance. Banks, governments and funds all use them as their
safest asset. So far, every time America has put its bonds up
for sale, the world has shown up to buy them.
So America is safe, right?
[pause]
Not quite.
Because every one of those advantages depends on one
thing: trust. And trust doesn't disappear slowly. It disappears
all at once.

Part 5: Could America fall like Rome?

People love comparing America to the Roman Empire. And
it's easy to see why.
But a modern collapse wouldn't look like barbarians at the
gates. It would look like a slow chain reaction.
Deficits that never stop. The CBO projects the yearly deficit
growing from about $2 trillion today to $3.1 trillion by 2036.
Interest rates that keep climbing. Politicians forced into
impossible choices: raise taxes, cut benefits, or borrow even
more. Then slower growth. Stubborn inflation. Investors
losing faith in Treasuries.
And here's a date to remember: 2030. That's when, in the
CBO's projections, America's debt breaks the record set
right after World War II.
And finally, the quiet part. America's financial superpower
status starts slipping away. Not in a day. But year by year,
until one day people realise the world has moved on.
[pause]
Is that guaranteed? No. Smart reforms, stronger growth, or
tough decisions could still turn this around. But every year
that nothing changes, the window gets a little smaller.

Conclusion

So, will America collapse because of its debt?
Here's the honest answer. There are three very different
futures: America going bankrupt, America going through a

painful financial crisis, or America slowly losing its crown as
the world's financial superpower.
The first is unlikely. The second is possible. And the third?
That's the one worth watching.
The CBO projects the debt held by the public rising from
about 101% of the economy this year to 120% by 2036,
higher than at any point in American history. Interest costs
are on track to nearly double, to $2.1 trillion a year. Nobody
can tell you the exact date when things break. But the
direction is clear.
The real question isn't whether America can borrow. It's how
long the world will keep believing it should.
